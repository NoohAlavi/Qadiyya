How to Use Qaḍiyya

from a blank page to a finished argument chart

Getting started Building the chart Premise types Sub-arguments The tree Notes Your arguments Export & backup Settings

1 Getting started

Qaḍiyya turns an argument into a chart: a conclusion, supported by numbered premises, each of which is either accepted as basic or proved by an argument of its own. It follows the chart format of classical manṭiq and kalām.

  1. On the home page, press New Argument and type what the argument is trying to prove. That becomes the title.
  2. You land in the editor, with a small starting chart already in place for you to overwrite.
  3. Your work saves as you go. There is no Save button.

No account is needed. Your arguments are tied to this browser, so see Export & backup to keep a copy.

2 Building the chart

Each row of the table is one statement. The last row is the conclusion, marked C; the rows above it are its premises, P1, P2, and so on.

#SkeletonPremiseType
P1All A is BEvery physical body occupies space.Self evident
P2C is AA stone is a physical body.Observational
CTherefore, C is BTherefore, a stone occupies space.
  • Skeleton (the second column): the statement reduced to letters, such as All A is B. It shows the logical shape of the premise.
  • Premise (the third column): the statement written out in words. Click any cell and type.
  • Add Premise (the button under the table) adds another premise to the argument.
  • ✕ deletes a premise. If it has sub-premises under it, you are asked to confirm first, because they are deleted with it.
  • Numbers (P1, P2…) are assigned automatically and update themselves whenever you add or delete.

3 Premise types

The dropdown on each premise says how you know it. This is what lets you trace an argument back to the premises where it can, and cannot, be questioned.

Inferential naẓarī نظري
The premise is not accepted on its own: it needs an argument to establish it. Choosing this opens a sub-argument for it (see the next section).

Non-inferential premises ḍarūrī ضروري

These need no further argument: the mind accepts them directly, in one of six ways. The tool shows eight options, because observational premises are split by which senses are used.

Self-evident awwaliyyāt الأوليات
The mind sees it is true as soon as it understands the terms, with no senses or evidence needed. Example: a whole is greater than its part.
Observational mushāhadāt المشاهدات
The mind accepts it on the testimony of the senses. This comes in two kinds:
Empirically observed ḥissiyyāt الحسيات
Observed through the outer senses: sight, hearing, touch, taste and smell. Example: this stone is rough.
Introspectively observed wijdāniyyāt الوجدانيات
Observed through the inner senses: what you perceive within yourself, such as pain, hunger or fear. Example: I feel thirsty.
Tested tajribiyyāt التجريبيات
Known through repeated observation: after seeing the same thing again and again, the mind becomes certain of the pattern. Example: paracetamol brings down a fever.
Intuited ḥadsiyyāt الحدسيات
Known by a sudden realization: many separate pieces of knowledge point one way, and the mind sees that they must have a particular cause. Example: the moon's light comes from the sun.
Mass-testified mutawātirāt المتواترات
Known because it is reported by so many eyewitnesses that, given how people behave, they could not all have conspired to lie. Example: Makkah exists, even if you have never seen it.
Subconsciously inferred qaḍāyā qiyāsātuhā maʿahā قضايا قياساتها معها
Known by way of a middle premise that comes to mind automatically whenever the terms are thought of, so the conclusion feels immediate. Example: four is an even number, because it can be divided into two equal parts.

These definitions follow the classification in the Isāghūjī as taught by Shaykh Hamza Karamali in his Kalām course, put here in simplified wording.

4 Sub-arguments

When you mark a premise Inferential, a new table appears beneath the main one with that premise as its conclusion. Fill in its premises the same way. Sub-arguments can go as deep as you need.

  • ↓ view sub-argument, shown beside an inferential premise, scrolls straight to its table.
  • The ▲ beside a sub-argument's title folds it away or opens it again. Qaḍiyya remembers which ones you folded.

5 The argument tree

At the bottom of the page, the Argument Tree draws the whole argument as one picture: premises feed into the conclusions they support, all the way to the main conclusion. It is read-only, and it redraws as you edit.

  • Boxes are coloured by type: green for non-inferential premises, gold for inferential ones, and a neutral box for the conclusion.
  • Click a box to jump to that premise's row in the table.
  • ⇅ Conclusion at bottom / top flips the tree so the conclusion sits at the other end.
  • The tree starts closed to keep the editor uncluttered. Press the ▼ beside its heading to open it, and ▲ to close it again. Qaḍiyya remembers this for each argument.

6 Notes

Under each premise's type is a small annotations… field. Use it to add detail, record a source, or write a critique.

Notes also appear in the tree as footnotes: a numbered gold badge on the box, and the full text in a Notes list beneath the tree. Click a badge to jump to its note, or a note to highlight its box. You can turn this off in the settings.

7 Your arguments

The My Arguments page lists everything you have made. From there you can:

  • Open an argument to work on it, or start a New Argument.
  • Rename it, duplicate it (a good way to try a different line of reasoning), or delete it.
  • Import JSON to bring in an argument you exported earlier, or one someone sent you. It is always added as a new argument, so nothing you have is overwritten.

You can also rename the current argument from inside the editor with the ✎ beside its title.

8 Export & backup

The Export button at the bottom right of the editor offers:

  • PDF (recommended): a paginated A4 document, good for sharing or printing.
  • Image (PNG): the argument tables as a single picture.
  • Tree (PNG): just the tree, with its footnotes.
  • JSON: the argument itself, which you can import again later or send to someone else.

Keep a backup. Arguments live in your browser's session, not in an account. Clearing cookies, switching browsers or devices, or sometimes a browser's own cleanup can start you with an empty workspace. The Download all arguments button in the settings saves everything in one file; restore it with Import JSON.

9 Settings

The gear ⚙ in the navigation bar opens the settings. Changes apply immediately.

  • Whether the tree opens by default, and whether its conclusion sits at the bottom or top.
  • Whether notes appear under the tree.
  • Whether each box in the tree shows its premise number (P1, P2 … C).
  • Whether sub-arguments open by default.
  • Text size for the tables and tree: small, medium or large.

A default only applies to sections you haven't opened or closed yourself. Make every argument follow them clears those individual choices.
